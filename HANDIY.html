01The verdict in one screen

Angela, the short answer: the idea is fully buildable on this laptop, and nothing found on the Internet blocks it. Several products already move a cursor from a webcam, but none covers the whole brief. The hard limits are the camera and Windows session rules, not the GPU.

Is there a complete solution?
Partly

UCL MotionInput, HandX and Virtual Touch Screen 2 already drive a cursor from a webcam. Ultraleap TouchFree does it with its own infrared camera. None combines trainable gestures, GPU-level latency, a supervised always-on service and control of elevated windows. Build it, but reuse their models and lessons.

Biggest latency term
The camera

Your built-in webcam recorded at 30 fps. Waiting for and reading a frame costs roughly 25–45 ms before any code runs. The RTX 4070 finishes both hand networks in about 1–2 ms (est.). Buy frames, not FLOPs: a 120 fps global-shutter camera removes more latency than any GPU trick.

Biggest impediment
Session 0

A true Windows service runs in Session 0. It cannot use your desktop, and its input does not reach your apps. Handiy therefore runs as a user-session agent that a small service starts and supervises. To control administrator windows it also needs uiAccess (a signed binary in Program Files).

Recommended stack
C++ + TensorRT

WinRT frame reader in real-time mode → one fused CUDA kernel → TensorRT FP16 hand and pose networks → tiny gesture networks → SendInput and Core Audio. Python is used only for the training Studio and for the first prototype.

Expected glass-to-cursor latency
≈ 65 → ≈ 18 ms

Est.: built-in 30 fps camera ≈ 55–75 ms. A 120 fps global-shutter camera ≈ 15–22 ms, or about 10 ms as felt with 8 ms of motion prediction. Section 04 has a calculator you can play with.

Five decisions that matter most

  1. Find the hand once, then track it. Run the palm detector only when the hand is lost; in steady state, one tiny landmark network per hand runs on a crop around the previous position. This is MediaPipe's own design, and it is the reason hand tracking can be this cheap.
  2. Never queue frames. Always process the newest frame and drop older ones (WinRT MediaFrameReader in Realtime mode, or a latest-frame slot). A queue of three frames at 30 fps silently adds 100 ms.
  3. Decouple the pointer from the camera. A 240 Hz pointer thread predicts between camera frames, so the cursor glides on your 240 Hz panel instead of jumping 30 times a second.
  4. Use the body for big gestures. Decide "hand raised" from the pose (wrist above nose or shoulder line). Pose survives the motion blur and the fingers leaving the frame, and your video shows both.
  5. Make every action deliberate. An engage state, hysteresis, cool-downs and a kill gesture defeat the "Midas touch" problem, where every movement you make becomes a command.

What the clip teaches the design

Lesson 1

Motion blur at 30 fps

During the fastest part of the rise, finger detail is smeared. Hand-landmark networks lose the hand exactly when it moves fastest. Fix: a shorter fixed exposure (more light, or infrared), decide big gestures from the wrist trajectory in the pose, and bridge 2–3 lost frames with prediction.

Lesson 2

Fingers leave the frame

A laptop webcam has a limited vertical field of view; a raised hand exits at the top. Fix: judge "raised" with the wrist and elbow from the pose network, with the face as a reference, never with fingertips alone.

Lesson 3

Backlight

The bright window on the left makes auto-exposure lengthen the exposure for your face, which adds blur. In dim rooms, many webcams also drop to 15 fps. Fix: while Handiy runs, lock a short exposure profile, add front light, or use the IR stream.

Lesson 4

The gesture's shape

Open palm rises, holds, closes into a fist, drops. That reads naturally as "raise to increase, close the fist to finish". Section 09 turns it into two exact designs with thresholds.

Lesson 5

Different budgets

A 1.5 s volume gesture is not latency-critical; the cursor is. Handiy gives each gesture its own budget: cursor < 20 ms of processing, discrete gestures confirmed within 100–150 ms of their end.

Blur assumes a hand 60 cm from a 70° camera, imaged 640 px wide. Prediction can hide smooth motion, never sudden starts or stops.

Why GPU speed barely matters for the cursor. At 30 fps the gap between two frames (33 ms) is about 20 times the inference time. Halving inference saves ≈ 1 ms; doubling the frame rate saves ≈ 15–25 ms. The first purchase for cursor-grade latency is a faster camera, not a faster GPU.

Where the RTX does matter. It makes robustness cheap: full-size landmark models, both hands, pose on every frame, and a spare 12× headroom for 120–240 fps cameras. The CPU path costs a few milliseconds per frame (est.) and cannot keep up with a 120 fps camera together with pose.

Blur, in numbers (est.). At 60 cm a 70° webcam sees about 84 cm of width. A hand moving at 1.5 m/s with a 16 ms exposure smears 2.4 cm, about 55 px at 1080p. With a 2 ms exposure it smears 3 mm, about 7 px. Short exposure needs light: a small 850 nm IR illuminator with a mono global-shutter camera solves both problems without dazzling you.

Why the typical GitHub "virtual mouse" feels laggy. The same five mistakes appear again and again:

  1. pyautogui sleeps 0.1 s after every call by default (pyautogui.PAUSE = 0.1). Each cursor move adds 100 ms.
  2. cv2.VideoCapture.read() returns frames from an internal queue, so the code often processes a stale frame.
  3. cv2.imshow + waitKey sit in the hot loop and add a frame or more.
  4. Jitter is "fixed" with a heavy moving average or a smoothing divisor, which adds 3–5 frames of lag (100–170 ms at 30 fps).
  5. Full 1080p frames are processed on the CPU, and Python's garbage collector pauses at random.

07Architecture

Three views: which processes exist and where they live, how one frame becomes a cursor move, and which thread does what in time.

7.1 Processes and sessions

  • HandiyAgent.exe runs once per interactive user session, at medium integrity, with uiAccess="true" in its manifest. It owns the camera, the CUDA context, the overlay and the effectors.
  • HandiySvc (LocalSystem, Automatic, delayed start) listens for session events, starts the agent in the active session, restarts it after a crash with back-off, and applies updates. It never touches frames.
  • HandiyStudio is a separate process for recording and training. It talks to the agent over a named pipe whose ACL admits only SYSTEM and the signed-in user, and it can pause the live agent while it uses the camera.
  • Storage. Signed, hash-checked models live in %ProgramData%\Handiy\models; per-user profiles and gesture samples (landmarks only, never images) in %LOCALAPPDATA%\Handiy.

7.2 One frame, end to end

  • Zero queues. The frame reader drops what it cannot deliver, and the slot keeps only the newest frame. A slow stage can never build up a backlog.
  • GPU-resident pre-processing. One kernel converts NV12 to RGB, warps the rotated hand ROI (as MediaPipe does), letterboxes, normalises and writes FP16. No CPU resize, no OpenCV in the hot path.
  • Track, don't re-detect. The next ROI comes from this frame's landmarks. The palm detector runs only when presence drops below a threshold, plus a slow low-priority scan (every 15 frames) to notice a second hand.
  • Pose on its own clock. Body keypoints change slowly relative to fingers, so pose runs at ≥ 30 Hz on a downscaled full frame. It feeds the "raised hand" logic and gives the tracker a fallback when the hand blurs.
  • The gesture classifier runs on the CPU. A 63→128→64→K network needs about 17 k multiply-adds, a few microseconds. Launching a GPU kernel would take longer.

Rules that keep it trustworthy

  • Engage first. A hand that wanders into view does nothing until it holds an engage shape inside the active zone.
  • Active zone. A box at desk height, calibrated in the Studio. Hands outside it are ignored, so reaching for a cup is safe.
  • Hysteresis everywhere. Entering a state needs a stronger signal than staying in it, so states never flicker.
  • N-of-M voting. Discrete gestures need 2–4 agreeing frames out of the last few.
  • Cool-downs. One swipe gives one "next track", never three.
  • Arbitration. When two gestures fire together, a fixed priority wins: kill > click > mode change > continuous.

Rules that keep it personal

  • One owner. Only the largest hand in the zone, attached (through pose) to the nearest body, controls the PC; other people in the room are ignored.
  • Handedness. Left-handed mode mirrors every gesture.
  • Per-app profiles. In PowerPoint a swipe changes the slide; in Spotify it changes the track.
  • Typing guard. If the real keyboard or mouse was used in the last 300 ms, pointer gestures are suppressed.
  • Visible feedback. Every state change shows on the overlay; a mode never runs silently.
  • Undo. A volume change shows its OSD for 1.5 s, and the opposite gesture reverts it.

  1. Pick a stable point. Steer with the knuckle area (blend of landmarks 0, 5 and 9), not the fingertip. The fingertip jitters most and moves when you pinch.
  2. Filter in camera space. Apply the One Euro filter to the normalised point before mapping. Starting values: min_cutoff = 1.0 Hz, beta = 0.007, d_cutoff = 1.0 Hz; tune them live.
  3. Speed-dependent gain. gain(v) = gmin + (gmax − gmin) · smoothstep(vlo, vhi, v). Slow hand ≈ 1–1.5 for precision; fast hand ≈ 6–10 to cross the screen.
  4. Click without the jump. A pinch moves the hand a little, so the cursor slips just before the click. Keep a ring buffer of the last 32 cursor positions; at pinch onset, click at the position from 80 ms earlier and freeze the pointer for 120 ms.
  5. Predict, carefully. A constant-velocity Kalman filter extrapolates by at most one camera frame. Prediction turns off during pinches and when the hand decelerates sharply, so the cursor does not overshoot on stops.
  6. Sub-pixel accumulation. Keep the fractional remainder between moves; otherwise slow motion stalls and then jumps.
  7. Multi-monitor and DPI. Declare per-monitor DPI awareness v2 and send absolute coordinates normalised to the whole virtual desktop. Display scaling (for example 150 %) then cannot shift the target.
  8. Edge help. Mild magnetism near screen edges and the taskbar; an optional dwell click for accessibility.

How good is good? Measure throughput (bits per second) with the ISO 9241-411 multi-directional tapping test. A desktop mouse typically scores about 4–5 bits/s; mid-air pointing usually scores lower. Track Handiy's score release by release.

11The low-latency checklist

Camera

  • Prefer the highest frame rate at a modest resolution (640×480 or 720p at 60+ fps) over 1080p30.
  • Lock exposure while Handiy runs (≤ 4 ms if the light allows); disable auto-exposure priority.
  • Set the power-line frequency to 60 Hz (Mexico) to avoid flicker banding.
  • Turn off automatic framing and other Studio Effects for this camera.
  • Prefer uncompressed NV12/YUY2 or Y8 when USB bandwidth allows; MJPEG adds decode time.

Capture

  • MediaFrameReader with AcquisitionMode::Realtime; at most one frame in flight.
  • Stamp every frame with SystemRelativeTime and drop any frame older than one frame period.
  • Register capture buffers with cudaHostRegister so the copy to the GPU is direct.
  • Never call imshow, save images or log per pixel in the hot path.

GPU

  • TensorRT FP16 engines with fixed shapes, built at install time, never at logon.
  • One CUDA graph per pipeline variant; highest-priority non-blocking stream.
  • Call cudaStreamQuery right after launching so the WDDM driver submits the work immediately instead of batching it.
  • One event wait per frame; copy back about 1 KB, nothing more.
  • Keep the context warm while a hand is present; let the RTX sleep when idle.
  • Optional on AC: "Prefer maximum performance" for HandiyAgent.exe in the NVIDIA control panel, so low GPU load does not lower clocks (measure first).

CPU & Windows

  • MMCSS for capture and pointer threads (AvSetMmThreadCharacteristicsW).
  • Opt hot threads out of power throttling (EcoQoS) with SetThreadInformation.
  • High-resolution waitable timers; never Sleep(1) loops.
  • No heap allocation, no locks shared with the UI, lock-free SPSC queues.
  • Logging goes to a ring buffer flushed by a low-priority thread.
  • Above-normal priority only on tier S; Normal everywhere else.

Effectors

  • One SendInput call per event, with move and button packed together.
  • Skip moves smaller than a whole pixel; keep the remainder.
  • Audio, brightness and media calls go to the worker thread, with cached COM objects.
  • React to default-device changes instead of re-opening the device per call.

Display & measurement

  • Use the 240 Hz internal panel; the 60 Hz external monitor adds about 12 ms.
  • Let Windows draw the hardware cursor; never draw a fake cursor in the overlay.
  • Keep per-stage timing histograms always on (each sample costs well under a microsecond) and show them in the Studio.

13Security, privacy & safety

Privacy

  • Frames live only in a ring of three RAM buffers, overwritten continuously, never written to disk.
  • The Studio stores landmarks (63 numbers per hand per frame), not pictures. Saving a debug clip is an explicit, per-session opt-in to a folder you choose.
  • No network listener and no telemetry.

Integrity

  • Models are listed in a signed SHA-256 manifest; the agent refuses anything else.
  • The named pipe is per user, with an ACL for SYSTEM and that user only; messages are validated against a schema and size-limited.
  • There is no "run this command" message on the pipe.

Allowed actions

  • Default allowlist: cursor, clicks, scroll, volume, media, brightness, windows and desktops.
  • "Launch program" and "type text" are off unless a profile enables them.
  • Rate limits and a volume ceiling (for example 85 %) protect your ears.

Kill switches

  • Ctrl+Alt+H (RegisterHotKey), the two-hand "T" gesture, and the tray toggle.
  • A small "camera live" indicator in the overlay, in addition to Windows' own camera indicator.
  • Pointer gestures pause automatically while you use the real keyboard or mouse.

Spoofing

  • A video of hands on another screen could trigger gestures. Pose association (the hand must belong to the nearest body) blocks most cases.
  • Optional IR-only mode: an infrared camera usually sees LCD screens as dark, which resists replay (est.; verify with your camera).
  • Optional local owner check by face embedding, off by default for privacy.

Accessibility

  • Dwell click, sticky drag, larger targets in the overlay, high-contrast OSD.
  • Every gesture can be re-recorded to fit the user's range of motion.
  • Works alongside Voice Access and Eye Control.

14Teaching Handiy new gestures

"Trained gestures" means you record a few examples and Handiy learns them in seconds. The Studio does the work; the live agent only receives a new, verified model.

Recording protocol

  • Static gestures: 5 sessions × 20 samples, in different light (window, lamp, evening), at 45, 60 and 90 cm, with and without glasses.
  • Dynamic gestures: 30 repetitions at slow, normal and fast speed.
  • Negatives: at least 10 minutes of ordinary activity: typing, phone, drinking, scratching your head, talking with your hands. This one recording decides the false-activation rate more than any model choice.
  • Only landmarks are saved, never images, unless you opt in.

Features

  • Static: landmarks relative to the wrist, divided by palm size, optionally rotated upright; 15 finger-curl angles; fingertip distances. Keep orientation for gestures that need it (thumb up vs thumb down).
  • Dynamic: static features per frame + wrist and fingertip velocities + pose features (wrist height between shoulder and nose, elbow angle).
  • Resample to a fixed 60 Hz time base so one model works with 30 and 120 fps cameras.

Models

  • Static: MLP 63 → 128 → 64 → K (< 20 k parameters), on the CPU.
  • Dynamic: causal TCN (dilated 1-D convolutions, about 0.8 s receptive field) or GRU(64), producing an answer every frame.
  • Few-shot templates: $Q or DTW on the 2-D wrist path for "draw a circle" or letters, from 3 examples.
  • Quick add: embedding + nearest neighbours adds a static gesture from 5 examples with no retraining.

Saying "I don't know"

  • An explicit none class (MediaPipe Model Maker requires one too).
  • Confidence threshold plus a distance-to-class check in embedding space, so unknown shapes are rejected.
  • Thresholds are chosen on the negatives to hit ≤ 1 false activation per hour, then recall is measured on held-out sessions.
  • "That was wrong" and "missed it" hotkeys label the last 2 s for the next training round.

15.3 One Euro filter (C++)

// One Euro filter (Casiez, Roussel & Vogel, CHI 2012). One instance per coordinate.
struct LowPass {
    double y = 0; bool init = false;
    double operator()(double x, double a) { y = init ? a * x + (1 - a) * y : x; init = true; return y; }
};

struct OneEuro {
    double minCutoff = 1.0, beta = 0.007, dCutoff = 1.0;    // tune live in the Studio
    LowPass x, dx;
    static double Alpha(double cutoff, double dt) {
        const double tau = 1.0 / (2.0 * 3.14159265358979 * cutoff);
        return 1.0 / (1.0 + tau / dt);
    }
    double operator()(double value, double dt) {              // dt in seconds, from frame timestamps
        const double d  = x.init ? (value - x.y) / dt : 0.0;
        const double ed = dx(d, Alpha(dCutoff, dt));
        return x(value, Alpha(minCutoff + beta * std::abs(ed), dt));
    }
};

{

import threading
import time

import cv2


class LatestFrame:
    """Grabber thread that keeps only the newest frame: never a queue."""

    def __init__(self, index=0, w=640, h=480, fps=60):
        self.cap = cv2.VideoCapture(index, cv2.CAP_MSMF)
        self.cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_FRAME_WIDTH, w)
        self.cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_FRAME_HEIGHT, h)
        self.cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_FPS, fps)
        self.frame, self.stamp = None, 0.0
        self.lock = threading.Lock()
        threading.Thread(target=self._run, daemon=True).start()

    def _run(self):
        while True:
            ok, f = self.cap.read()
            if ok:
                with self.lock:
                    self.frame, self.stamp = f, time.perf_counter()

    def newest(self):
        with self.lock:
            return self.frame, self.stamp

18Measuring & testing

Latency rig

A small microcontroller (ESP32 or Arduino) blinks an LED inside the camera view and starts a microsecond timer. In debug mode Handiy turns a corner of the overlay white when it sees the LED; a photodiode taped on the screen stops the timer. That is the true glass-to-glass time, frame by frame.

Quick method: film your hand and the screen with a phone at 240 fps and count frames between the hand stopping and the cursor stopping (≈ 4 ms resolution).

Internal timing

Every frame carries its capture timestamp (SystemRelativeTime, QPC-based). Each stage adds its own timestamp, and the agent keeps histograms of each stage and of capture-to-SendInput. The Studio shows median, p95 and p99 live.

Replay harness

Recorded landmark streams (or videos) with labelled gesture intervals are replayed through the full pipeline without a camera. The results are deterministic, so the classifiers and the state machine get real regression tests. Following Angela's rule, the harness runs in a visible window with the overlay on screen.

Pointing quality

ISO 9241-411 multi-directional tapping gives throughput in bits per second. Run it for the mouse, the touchpad and Handiy on the same day, and track it per release.

False-activation soak

Eight hours of ordinary work with Handiy armed; every unintended action is logged with the two seconds of landmarks before it. Target: at most one.

Robustness matrix

Window backlight, lamp, dark room with IR; 45 / 60 / 90 cm; sleeves, glasses, a second person in the frame; Teams holding the camera; lock/unlock, sleep/resume, AC/battery, external monitor; forced tier C.

20Optional: a bridge to Tlamatini

Handiy stands alone in v1. Later it can publish events, never frames on a local named pipe, and Tlamatini can react to them.

// one JSON line per event on \\.\pipe\handiy-events (ACL: SYSTEM + the signed-in user)

  • Raise a hand to talk: a gesture presses Tlamatini's chat microphone button and starts dictation.
  • Flows: a swipe advances a Prompt Flow step; a custom gesture starts a saved .flw.
  • Voice feedback: Talker can confirm "volume 64 %" when the screen is out of sight.
  • Safety rule: a gesture must never approve an Ask-Execs permission prompt. Approvals stay with the keyboard and mouse.
  • If Handiy ever becomes a Tlamatini workflow agent, its display name is Handiy and its directory is handiy, following the agent-creation runbook.
